How to Make Paper Snowflakes: Patterns and Folding Techniques That Work

By EventBuzz Team • October 21, 2026 • 10 min read

Everyone remembers cutting paper snowflakes as a child, and most adults also remember the disappointment of unfolding a chunky paper doily with four points instead of a snowflake’s six. The difference between the doily and the delicate lacework you imagined is almost never talent; it is the fold. Real snowflakes have six fold symmetry, and paper ones only get it when the paper is folded the right way. This tutorial on how to make paper snowflakes teaches that fold step by step, the cutting shapes that produce lace, and patterns from beginner to intricate.

Snowflakes are the fastest decoration in the December craft box: one sheet, one pair of scissors, five minutes, and a window transformed. They pair naturally with the other paper crafts in our coloring pages guide on a family craft afternoon, and they feed directly into the window displays in our Christmas window decorations guide. Gather paper and scissors, and fold along.

The One Rule: Six Points Come From Six Folds

Every real snow crystal grows with six sides, and that six fold symmetry is what your eyes recognize as snowflake rather than flower. A paper snowflake folded in quarters, the way most people instinctively fold, unfolds with four or eight points and reads as a doily no matter how prettily you cut it. The method below folds the paper into sixths, so every cut you make is repeated six times around the center, and the result looks right before you have any skill at all.

Once the fold is right, cutting is forgiving. Snowflakes are an abstract art: there is no wrong shape, only shapes that connect or fall apart. Keep one principle in mind while cutting, which is that the folded edges are the skeleton of the design. Cut into them boldly for pattern, but never cut a folded edge completely away, or the snowflake separates into confetti at the unfold, a mistake every crafter makes exactly once.

Scissors matter more than paper. A sharp pair with a pointed tip gives control in tight corners; blunt safety scissors are right for small children, with an adult doing any intricate work. Six folded layers get thick near the point, so small cuts and patience beat brute force, and if your hand tires, you are probably cutting too much at once. A hole punch adds perfect circles and is a kind tool for beginners who cannot yet cut a smooth curve.

The Six Fold Method, Step by Step

  1. Start with a square. From a rectangular sheet, fold one corner diagonally to the opposite edge and trim the excess strip, or buy square origami style paper. Coffee filters skip this step entirely.
  2. Fold the triangle in half by bringing the two sharp corners together, making a smaller triangle, and crease firmly.
  3. Divide this triangle into thirds. Fold the right third over toward the left at roughly a sixty degree angle, then fold the left third over it the same way, so the edges line up as evenly as you can. This is the step that creates sixths, and near enough is genuinely good enough.
  4. Trim the uneven tail ends straight across so the folded packet has a clean top edge.
  5. Fold the packet in half lengthwise if you want twelve layer intricacy, or leave it as is for easier cutting for children.
  6. Cut your pattern into both folded edges and the point, then unfold gently, one layer at a time.

If your thirds fold looks untidy the first time, unfold and refold it once; the creases from the first attempt actually guide the second. The most common beginner error is folding the thirds unevenly, which produces one fat point and one thin one on the finished flake, charming rather than ruined. Perfection arrives around flake four, which is why this craft pairs so well with the patience building pages in our coloring pages guide on the same table.

Cutting Basics: The Five Shapes That Make Lace

Nearly every beautiful snowflake is built from five cuts: the long triangle notch, which makes the dramatic points; the small triangle bite, which makes sparkle along the arms; the curve or scallop, which softens edges into lace; the slit, a straight snip that opens into a diamond when unfolded; and the circle punch, which makes clean rings and dots. Combine two or three of them per arm and the design already looks deliberate, because repetition around six points multiplies simple cuts into complexity.

Cut most heavily along the outer folded edge, which becomes the snowflake’s outer lace, and more sparingly near the point, which becomes the center. A tiny snip at the very point makes the classic center hole or star. Keep bridges of paper between cuts, especially on the folded edges; a snowflake is a structure, and the prettiest ones hold a surprising amount of negative space without collapsing because their skeleton was respected.

Three Patterns to Try: Beginner, Classic and Advanced

Beginner pattern: along the outer edge, cut three deep triangle notches; along each folded side, cut two small triangles; snip the point flat. The result is a bold, graphic flake with strong points, genuinely handsome and almost impossible to ruin, which is exactly what a first attempt should be. Classic lace pattern: add scallop curves between the notches, a slit near the point, and small bites marching up both folded edges, and the flake turns properly lacy while staying sturdy enough for window display.

Advanced pattern: work in fine detail: thin parallel slits that unfold into stripes, tiny triangles in rows, a punched circle ring, and one daring deep cut that nearly reaches the center fold. Advanced flakes reward slow cutting and sharp scissors, and they are the ones worth pressing under a book overnight and framing. Make one hero flake per session rather than a batch, and display it where light passes through it, as suggested in our window decorations guide.

Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Folding in quarters produces the four pointed doily; refold in sixths using the thirds step. Cutting off a folded edge entirely separates the flake; rescue the pieces as individual snow crystals on a window, where nobody can tell. Folds too thick to cut means the paper is too heavy or you folded one time too many; switch to thinner paper and skip the final lengthwise fold. Flakes that will not open flat need pressing under a heavy book overnight, or a whisper of a cool iron through a protective cloth, by an adult.

Asymmetry that bothers you usually traces to the uneven thirds fold; measure the thirds step by eye against the triangle’s edge and refold rather than forcing it. And a universal truth worth teaching children at the table: no two of your flakes will match, which is not a flaw in the method but the most scientifically accurate thing about the entire craft.

Displaying Snowflakes: Windows, Garlands and Gifts

Windows are the classic gallery because daylight turns paper into lace. Attach flakes with a tiny loop of removable tape or a dot of reusable adhesive at the points, and cluster them at different heights as if falling. A garland threads flakes along string or fine ribbon through opposite points, spaced so they can turn slightly; hang it across a mantel, a doorway or a child’s bedroom window using the styling ideas in our DIY decorations guide.

Smaller sturdy flakes in card or foil paper become gift toppers, place cards and tree ornaments, hanging happily beside the felt and wood pieces in our DIY ornaments guide. A mobile of flakes on threads at different lengths above a table turns slowly in warm air and delights toddlers. Whatever the display, keep paper flakes away from candles and open flames, and take them down gently to store flat between book pages for next year.

Snowflakes With Kids and in Classrooms

For young children, pre fold the paper and let them cut the big shapes, or start them on coffee filters, which are round, thin and forgiving. Sit new cutters between two experienced ones and let teaching happen sideways. In classrooms, a five minute demonstration of the thirds fold at the start saves thirty minutes of doilies, and one displayed hero flake gives the class a target that pulls everyone’s second attempt up a level, echoing the group craft spirit of our family activities guide.

Honest assessment: pros are the near zero cost, the instant results, the genuine skill it quietly teaches, and displays that cost nothing to store. Cons are paper scraps in every corner, which a tabletop bin largely solves, and the addictiveness of just one more flake, which no guide can help you with. Of all December crafts, this is the one where five spare minutes are always enough.

Conclusion

Paper snowflakes reward one small piece of knowledge: fold in sixths, and the lace takes care of itself. Learn the fold, cut triangles, scallops and slits with growing boldness, press your best work flat, and let windows and garlands fill with falling paper snow. It is the cheapest, fastest and most forgiving decoration of the season, and it still manages to feel like a small magic trick every single time you unfold one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my paper snowflakes have four points instead of six?

Because the paper was folded in quarters. Real snowflakes have six fold symmetry, so the paper must be folded into sixths: fold a square into a triangle, halve it, then fold it into thirds at sixty degree angles before cutting. That single change fixes the doily problem completely, even with very simple cuts.

What is the best paper for paper snowflakes?

Standard copy paper is the best all rounder: crisp, cheap and easy to cut through six layers. Coffee filters are ideal for young children because they are already round and thin. Tissue paper gives delicate translucent flakes but tears easily, and light card or foil paper makes sturdy flakes for ornaments and gift toppers.

How do I get my snowflakes to lie flat?

Unfold them gently, layer by layer, then press them under a heavy book overnight. For stubborn creases, an adult can press the flake briefly with a cool iron through a protective cloth. Storing flakes flat between the pages of a large book also keeps them perfect for next December.

What cuts make a snowflake look lacy?

Combine deep triangle notches on the outer edge for drama, small triangle bites along the folded edges for sparkle, scallop curves for softness, and one or two straight slits that open into diamonds. Keep bridges of paper between cuts so the structure holds, and snip the point for a center star or hole.

Can young children make real six point snowflakes?

Yes, with the folding done by an adult or older child. Coffee filters are the easiest start, being round and thin, and blunt safety scissors handle the big triangle cuts fine. Expect bold, graphic flakes rather than lace, celebrate them as first attempts, and let intricacy arrive with age and practice.
