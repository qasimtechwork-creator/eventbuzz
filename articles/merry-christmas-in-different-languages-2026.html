How to Say Merry Christmas in 40 Languages: A Festive Phrase Guide

By EventBuzz Team • October 21, 2026 • 9 min read

One greeting, forty ways to say it, and a whole world celebrating in the same week. Learning how to say Merry Christmas in different languages is part party trick, part genuine kindness: the right greeting in a colleague’s, neighbor’s or in law’s own language lands with a warmth that English sometimes cannot. This festive phrase guide gives you merry christmas in 40 languages, twenty two from Europe and eighteen from the rest of the world, with honest notes on pronunciation and the situations where each greeting shines.

Use this guide alongside our Christmas traditions around the world for the customs behind the words, and our wishes for family and wishes for friends when you want to build a full card message around your chosen phrase. Every greeting below is a well established, widely used form; where a language has several variants, we have chosen the most common one and said so.

How to Use This Guide (and a Word About Pronunciation)

Greetings are written the way you will most often see them, in their standard spelling, with simplified transliteration where the original script is not the Latin alphabet, as with Russian, Greek, Mandarin and Japanese. Stress and tones are simplified too: this guide gets you understood and appreciated, not accent perfect. The golden rule with any borrowed greeting is cheerful confidence; a warmly delivered approximation earns smiles everywhere, while a mumbled perfect rendering earns a request to repeat it.

A few languages greet the whole season rather than the day itself. Polish Wesołych Świąt literally wishes happy holidays, and it is the natural Christmas greeting in Poland. Swedish, Norwegian and Danish all share versions of God Jul, good Yule, a reminder that these celebrations are older than their current names. Notes like these are included because the phrase you choose for a card deserves a sentence of understanding behind it.

Listen for the family resemblances: the Scandinavian God Jul forms echo each other, the Romance languages carry their versions of Nativity and birth in words like Navidad, Natale and Natal, and the Slavic greetings, Czech, Polish, Croatian, Russian and Ukrainian, each turn on words for the feast or the birth itself. Russian and Ukrainian greetings here are given in transliteration; both wish a good Christmas by naming the Nativity. Greek Kalá Christoúgenna works the same way, a blessing on the birth of Christ, and is the standard greeting throughout Greece and Cyprus.

Amharic Melkam Gena carries a calendar surprise: Ethiopian Christmas, Genna, is celebrated on January 7 in the Ethiopian calendar tradition, as are the Christmases of several Orthodox churches, so the greeting may be exchanged well after December 25. Hawaiian Mele Kalikimaka began as a phonetic borrowing of the English phrase itself and became a beloved local classic. Filipino Maligayang Pasko comes from the Spanish Pascua, a legacy of the Philippines’ history, in a country famous for one of the world’s longest Christmas seasons.

Writing the Greeting in Cards and Messages

In a card, the borrowed greeting works best as the headline, with your English message beneath it doing the personal work: the phrase in their language, then your own words in yours. That pairing shows effort without gambling the whole card on your spelling. Check the spelling twice against the tables above before committing pen to card, especially the greetings with accents and special letters, like Polish, Czech and Vietnamese, where the marks are part of the word, not decoration.

For group cards and office messages, one greeting in the recipient’s language among English well wishes is a gracious touch that singles nobody out awkwardly; it celebrates rather than performs. If you are signing for a household in one of the card projects in our DIY Christmas cards guide, lettering a borrowed greeting as the card’s artwork is a beautiful, achievable design: one phrase, written large, with a translation in small letters beneath so everyone shares the meaning.

Thoughtful Use: Who Celebrates, and When

A borrowed greeting is a kindness, and kindness includes knowing the room. Christmas is celebrated by Christian communities in nearly every country on this list, and the greetings are welcomed by those who celebrate, including in countries where Christians are a minority with long local traditions, from India to Egypt to Japan, where a festive, largely secular Christmas is widely enjoyed. Where you are unsure whether someone celebrates, the season’s neutral good wishes are the respectful default, and nothing in this guide obliges you to guess.

Timing varies as much as language. Most of Europe and the Americas greet on December 24 and 25, Ethiopian and several Orthodox celebrations fall on January 7, and Armenian traditions celebrate in early January as well. A greeting delivered on the right day for the recipient’s tradition is the advanced move this whole guide quietly aims at: not just the right words, but the right words on their Christmas, which is a message of attention no translation app can send for you. The customs behind many of these dates are told in our traditions around the world guide.

From Phrase to Conversation: Three Ways to Go Further

First, learn the greeting of one person in your life properly, pronunciation and all, rather than forty greetings shallowly; depth impresses and delights in a way a list cannot. Second, pair the phrase with a question about how their family celebrates, and then actually listen: you will collect better stories in one December than a decade of small talk provides. Third, teach children one greeting a year as a family tradition, choosing the language of a friend, a heritage or next year’s holiday destination, and let the collection grow with them.

Our wider message collections can carry you the rest of the way once the headline phrase is chosen: wishes for coworkers for the office card, and the family and friends collections linked in the introduction for everyone else. However far you take it, the spirit that makes a borrowed greeting work is the same one that makes the season work: noticing other people carefully enough to meet them in their own words.

Conclusion

Forty greetings, one sentiment: joy to you and yours, in the words you grew up hearing it. Choose the languages of the people actually in your life, learn those few well, write them carefully in cards, and deliver them on the right day. Feliz Navidad, Joyeux Noël, God Jul, Mele Kalikimaka, and from all of them, in every language on this page: Merry Christmas.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you say Merry Christmas in Spanish and French?

In Spanish it is Feliz Navidad, the phrase the famous song made known worldwide. In French it is Joyeux Noël. Both are completely standard, safe for cards, colleagues and strangers alike, and both name the festival directly: Navidad and Noël each descend from words for the Nativity, the birth at the center of Christmas.

What is the most widely understood Christmas greeting in this list?

Feliz Navidad reaches furthest thanks to the song and the size of the Spanish speaking world, and Mele Kalikimaka is recognized far beyond Hawaii for the same musical reason. In practice, though, the most effective greeting is never the most famous one; it is the one in the language of the person standing in front of you.

Is it appropriate to use a Christmas greeting from another culture?

Yes, when it is offered warmly and to someone who celebrates Christmas, it reads as respect and effort. Use correct spelling in writing, deliver it cheerfully rather than as a performance, and be ready to say what it means. Where you do not know whether someone celebrates, neutral seasonal good wishes are the thoughtful default.

Why do some countries celebrate Christmas on January 7?

Several Orthodox churches, and the Ethiopian tradition, follow a different church calendar for the feast, placing Christmas Day on January 7 in the civil calendar. That is why greetings like the Amharic Melkam Gena and the Russian and Ukrainian forms in this guide may be exchanged in early January as well as late December.

How can I learn the pronunciation of a greeting?

This guide gives standard spellings and simplified transliterations, which get you understood. For pronunciation, listen to a native speaker recording of the single phrase and repeat it aloud a few times before the moment; greetings are short, and five honest repetitions beat silent reading. Cheerful confidence matters more than accent perfection everywhere on this list.
